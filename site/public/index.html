Why it exists

For months, on a real PC with its antivirus switched on and up to date, a program sat set to start at every sign-in. It was signed by a real software company, so it looked safe. Next to it were planted files: one changed after signing, three unsigned, and hidden payloads dressed up as sound files. Nothing flagged it. Hideout did, on its first scan — and flagged nothing else among 465 things set to start by themselves.

This guide is not an AI

It's a checklist and a letter writer, and it runs in your browser. It doesn't connect to anything, and what you type stays on this page.

The AI guide isn't switched on yet. When it is, this page will say so plainly, and you'll be able to tell which answers come from it.

If it's happening right now

Someone controlling your mouse, a caller on the phone, a pop-up demanding payment:

  1. Hang up, and stop typing any codes.
  2. Take this PC off the internet. Switch Wi-Fi off, or pull the cable out.
  3. Don't pay anything, don't buy gift cards, and don't call a number shown in a pop-up.

Then change your passwords from a different device you trust. Start with your email, then your bank.

The guide

Six steps to get through a scam, in order. Tick each one when you've done it. Your ticks are saved in this browser and nowhere else, and you can clear them whenever you like. Hideout never logs in to your accounts, never cancels anything and never sends email for you. You send your own letters and make your own bank calls.

  1. Stop the bleeding. End the remote session, change passwords from a clean device, turn on two-step sign-in. Then let Windows Security clean up.
  2. List what they took. Money sent, cards used, accounts touched, and the date it happened.
  3. Call your bank. On the number printed on your card — not a number from the caller. Say it was a scam.
  4. Check for still-charging. Any company charging again after you cancelled, with the cancel date and the charge date.
  5. Send the letters. One at a time. Keep copies.
  6. Watch for the second scam. Anyone offering to "recover" your money for a fee, a gift card or remote access is a scam. Only your bank, your card company or the official fraud-reporting service can help.

Write a letter

Fill in what you know and leave the rest. Anything this page can't know shows up in [brackets] for you to fill in. A [Confirm: …] line is a sentence to keep only if it's true. Nothing you type here is saved or sent anywhere.

Your letter will appear here. Read it, fill in the [brackets], delete any [Confirm: …] line that isn't true, and send it yourself. This page never sends anything.

Deeper checks you do yourself

This page can't look inside your PC or your accounts, so it can't tell you that anything is clean. Ticking a box means you checked it. A box you haven't ticked is just not checked yet.

What Hideout promises

It only looks

No deleting, moving, disabling or uploading. It can't change your PC, and neither can the guide.

Hands off your accounts

It never asks for passwords, never logs in anywhere, never cancels or sends anything for you.

Memory locked to your PC

Your case file and statements are stored encrypted, with a key sealed by your PC's security chip. Nothing is uploaded to be stored.

The guide sees less than you do

When you ask the guide, it gets a summary with your name, account numbers and PC name removed — and nothing else from your PC.

One door out

The app can reach exactly one address on the internet: the guide's service. Everything else is refused.

Open to check

The code is public. Anyone can read what it does — and what it doesn't.
