Why it exists

For months, on a real PC with its antivirus switched on and up to date, a program sat set to start at every sign-in. It was signed by a real software company, so it looked safe. Next to it were planted files: one changed after signing, three unsigned, and hidden payloads dressed up as sound files. Nothing flagged it. Hideout did, on its first scan — and flagged nothing else among 465 things set to start by themselves.

The guide — try it here

An AI assistant, powered by Claude, that walks you through what to do, one step at a time, in plain words. It says it's an AI, and it can be wrong. It remembers where you left off on this device only — ticking a step saves it here, in your browser, nothing is uploaded. You send the letters. Hideout never logs into your accounts, never cancels anything and never sends email for you.

  1. Stop the bleeding: end the remote session, change passwords from a clean device, turn on two-step sign-in.
  2. List what they took: money sent, cards used, accounts touched, and the date it happened.
  3. Call your bank on the number printed on your card — not a number from the caller. Say it was a scam.
  4. Check for still-charging: any company charging again after you cancelled, with the cancel date and the charge date.
  5. Send the letters below, one at a time. Keep copies.
  6. Watch for the second scam: anyone offering to "recover" your money for a fee or remote access is a scam.

Deeper checks — scam drift, leaks, rogue AI, and evidence

  • Scam drift, since the scam date: new scheduled tasks, new services, new programs set to start at sign-in, new users on the PC, password or recovery-email changes, new email forwarding rules, and new devices on your accounts. Check these yourself — the guide never logs in to check them for you.
  • Small test charges first: tiny charges, often $1 or less, before a large charge. Those are card-testing. Flag them for your bank with the same dates.
  • API and secret leaks — look, never paste: files in startup folders, Downloads, or Documents that contain keys, tokens, or seed phrases. If you find one, rotate it at its provider first, then delete the file. Never paste a key, token, password, or seed phrase into this guide, into email, or into chat. This page never asks for one, and a real guide never will.
  • Rogue AI and agents: AI browser extensions, agent tools, or remote-access programs installed around the scam date, and anything set to run by itself that can read your screen, files, or browser. If you did not install it, treat it as part of the scam until proven otherwise, and remove it through Windows Security, step by step.
  • Drifting evidence — preserve it: do not delete the suspicious files, emails, call logs, or transaction records before your bank or a report asks for them. Save screenshots, transaction IDs, and dates. A file changed after signing, a new folder with a machine-made name, and a task created after the scam date are evidence, not just clutter.
  • Browser drift: homepage or search changed, new extensions, saved passwords exported, or new bookmarks to fake bank sites. Reset those from your browser settings, on a clean device if you can.
  • Writes the letters: refund requests, cancellations, bank disputes. Anything it can't know from you goes in [brackets] for you to confirm or delete.
  • Warns about the second scam: anyone offering to "recover" your money for a fee or remote access.

What Hideout promises

It only looks

No deleting, moving, disabling or uploading. It can't change your PC, and neither can the guide.

Hands off your accounts

It never asks for passwords, never logs in anywhere, never cancels or sends anything for you.

Memory locked to your PC

Your case file and statements are stored encrypted, with a key sealed by your PC's security chip. Nothing is uploaded to be stored.

The guide sees less than you do

When you ask the guide, it gets a summary with your name, account numbers and PC name removed — and nothing else from your PC.

One door out

The app can reach exactly one address on the internet: the guide's service. Everything else is refused.

Open to check

The code is public. Anyone can read what it does — and what it doesn't.
